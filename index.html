// how it works

Fork, ship, merge back.

Each engagement runs like a branch. It exists to get one function working, then folds back into the team.

01 · forkOpen seat

Take the open chair

A company has urgent work and nobody assigned to it. Usually that's the sales motion or a product that needs to get to launch. I take ownership of it.

02 · shipIn progress

Build it and run it

I make the pieces myself, from the CRM to the launch plan, and keep running them until they work without me.

03 · mergeHanded off

Hand back the seat

When the founder or a full-time hire is ready to take over, they get a working function and a playbook written for them.

Taking a product to launch

A medical education app that was stuck before launch. I took it through to live users in a new market.

productProduct Lead
Kroot · product launch
Shipped
Product audit backlog
I walked the whole user flow, from onboarding through spaced repetition to payment, and logged every critical bug, UX problem, and gap between what was built and what was specced. The result was a prioritized backlog with severity ratings and acceptance criteria that developers could pick up directly.
Launch plan 90-day
I wrote a 90-day launch plan in phases, each with exit criteria the team had to meet before moving on. The work was split by workstream (product, content, development, and user research), and every workstream had an owner.
Payment strategy decision memo
I compared three ways to charge: App Store first, web first, or a hybrid. The memo covered build complexity, expected conversion on each path, regional compliance rules, and how long each would take to bring in revenue. The team went with web first.
User research 10 sessions
I ran 10 structured discovery sessions with medical students in the target market, including writing the discussion guide and recruiting participants. My brief afterward covered which subjects they prioritized, what they'd pay, and how they actually studied compared with what the team had assumed.
Closed beta 20 users
I ran a closed beta with 20 users, with structured feedback forms, a weekly synthesis, and bug triage by severity. Before going live I set the launch metrics (activation, 7-day and 30-day retention, and session frequency), which gave the team a baseline for the soft launch.

Reorganizing a national contact center network

A national clinical contact center network with 170+ sites was going through a major reorganization. I built the workforce and staffing models leadership used to plan it.

bizopsOrg design / workforce modeling
Contact center reorg · workforce modeling
Shipped
Workforce mapping data
I mapped the client's workforce data to its core service lines, then widened the scope to cover leadership and administrative support roles. Executive roles were left out, and data anomalies went to the client for review.
One staffing method everywhere fte
I applied a single full-time-equivalent staffing method across departments using regional network data. Clear inclusion rules meant every role was counted the same way in every service line.
Future-state staffing model
The model took a current state of 15 to 20 tracked positions per service down to a future-state shell of 4 to 5 positions per service, simple enough for leadership to plan with directly.
Regional consolidation 18 → 5
Leadership was consolidating 18 regional networks into 5, which meant facilities and their staff would move across new boundaries. I allocated headcount to the new regions using each facility's share of regional enrollment as a stand-in for demand.
Supervisor ratio scenarios excel
I built an adjustable Excel model that tested supervisor-to-staff ratios of 1:10, 1:12, and 1:16 and showed the supervisory surplus under each. Known data gaps, such as inconsistent role names, were written down so leadership knew where the numbers were soft. I then cleaned the model up for an executive audience and delivered it to the client's project sponsor.
Merged: leadership got 3 to 5 costed options

// business operations

Keeping the machine running

Operations work at early-stage startups and larger organizations, mostly the tasks that sit between teams.

open-chair / ops checks
Queued
  • I've run several workstreams at once at startups and larger companies: tracking status, running weekly standups, raising blockers early, and writing down decisions as they were made. Cross-team work tends to stall at handoffs, and this keeps it moving.
  • During one startup's growth push I wrote role scorecards, ran first-round screens, and gave the founder a framework for evaluating candidates. Hiring went from gut calls to a process with set criteria.
  • When a company parted ways with an outside dev team, I scoped what was still owed, reviewed the contract, and wrote every outgoing message. The handoff finished cleanly and the company's position stayed protected.
  • I've written playbooks for content production, QA, bug triage, and partner tracking. I write them for whoever inherits the process next, which is why they get used.
  • I've handled partner follow-ups, investor updates, and status reports for clients, sent on a regular schedule in a format the reader can act on right away.
  • I build simple reports on pipeline health, sprint completion, open action items, and budget against actuals, laid out for the person who has to read them.
